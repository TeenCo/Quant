# Source Grading and Evidence Policy

## Grade direction

**Lower is stronger.** Grade the evidence for a particular claim rather than the general reputation of the author, outlet, or platform. A primary source can be authoritative about its own API terms yet weak evidence that its data is complete or that a strategy works.

| Grade | Evidence class | Examples / use |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Exceptional | Recent peer-reviewed or official primary evidence; transparent, reproducible methods and data provenance; independent validation where appropriate. |
| 2 | Strong | Peer-reviewed work or robust primary-source research with clear methods, but some limits in replication, scope, or current applicability. |
| 3 | Credible | High-quality working paper, official documentation, or established technical research; not yet fully replicated or peer reviewed. |
| 4 | Useful with material caveat | Sound methodological exposition or strong practitioner material, but incomplete evidence, narrow sample, or clear external-validity caveat. |
| 5 | Discovery/reference | Provider/vendor documentation about its own product or dataset; useful for workflow and claims about features, not independent validation. |
| 6 | Limited | Secondary synthesis, technical exposition with weak/missing provenance, or an older study with uncertain applicability. |
| 7 | Weak lead | Trade writing, practitioner report, or exploratory analysis with incomplete methods/data. |
| 8 | Very weak lead | Promotional, selective, or hard-to-audit account with limited substantiation. |
| 9 | Anecdotal | Individual case, market lore, or story without systematic evidence. |
| 10 | Unverified | Unsupported assertion, opaque origin, or claim not yet checked. Preserve only as a lead. |

## Keep these dimensions separate

Every source note should carry:

- **Evidence grade (1–10):** support for the specific claim.
- **Recency:** publication/update date and whether the result's data or institutional details may be stale.
- **Novelty:** whether the idea or data angle is underexplored; novelty does not imply validity.
- **Source type:** peer-reviewed article, preprint, official data, vendor docs, practitioner account, media story, etc.
- **Claim scope:** exactly what the source supports and what it does not.

## Rules for market hypotheses

1. Treat any correlation or narrative as a hypothesis, not an edge.
2. Check the source and timestamp lineage, universe coverage, corporate actions, revisions, missingness, and known biases.
3. Preserve every trial, including null results, so later researchers can see the search process.
4. Adjust inference for serial dependence, selection, multiple testing, and overlapping labels.
5. Evaluate net returns after realistic execution, fees, financing, borrow, spread, and market impact.
6. Seek an economic mechanism and independent or out-of-time replication; neither replaces statistical checks.

## Applying grades consistently

Grade the exact claim, not a document as a whole. A regulator's endpoint documentation may be Grade 1 for stated API fields/access schedules, while completeness of the underlying measurement may remain unverified. A peer-reviewed paper can be strong evidence for a narrow sample-specific estimate but weaker for current markets or net tradability. A recent date does not upgrade evidence; an old foundational theorem need not be downgraded if its scope remains applicable.

When assigning a grade, write a one-sentence rationale answering: **what claim does this source support, and what prevents a stronger grade?** Keep platform access, data quality, economic mechanism, predictive relationship, and strategy performance as separate claims. Do not average grades across unlike claims.
