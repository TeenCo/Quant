# Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning (RL) studies sequential decisions where an agent observes a state, chooses an action, receives reward, and influences future states. Core concepts include Markov decision processes, policies, value functions, Bellman equations, exploration/exploitation, and off-policy evaluation.

## Potential finance framing

Portfolio execution, dynamic hedging, order placement, inventory control, and constrained rebalancing can be expressed as sequential decision problems. State/action/reward definitions must reflect actual information, feasible actions, and costs.

## Why naive trading RL fails

- A historical price simulator does not reproduce the market's response to the agent's own orders.
- Reward shaping can hide leverage, tail risk, or turnover costs.
- The same historical path is reused repeatedly during training/search.
- Randomized episodes can leak temporal and regime structure.
- High variance and reward hacking make model selection especially difficult.
- A backtest does not substitute for controlled prospective execution or safe off-policy evaluation.

## Minimum validation

Use a realistic execution simulator; include spread, impact, fees, financing, constraints, latency, and market response where relevant; compare simple fixed policies; use chronological episodes and multiple regimes; log all trials; stress state/action design; separate training from evaluation; report drawdown/tail risk and net utility.

## MDP formulation and Bellman equation

An MDP is specified by state \(s_t\), action \(a_t\), transition distribution \(P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)\), reward \(r_t\), and discount \(\gamma\). A policy \(\pi(a\mid s)\) aims to maximize expected discounted reward. The optimal value satisfies

\[
V^*(s)=\max_a\;\mathbb{E}[r(s,a,s')+\gamma V^*(s')\mid s,a].
\]

In market simulation, the transition distribution is usually estimated from historical data or a simulator that may not respond realistically to the agent. If the agent's action affects liquidity or price, replaying a fixed historical tape violates the assumption that the transition law is independent of the policy's impact.

## Example: execution reward design

For executing a parent order of target quantity \(Q\), a state might include remaining quantity, time remaining, current spread/depth, volatility, and market schedule. Actions could specify child-order size/aggressiveness. Reward can be negative implementation shortfall plus explicit penalties for unfilled quantity, inventory risk, and constraint violations. Specify a benchmark price and side consistently; otherwise reward can incentivize delaying execution or exploiting accounting artifacts. Include realistic partial fills, queue priority, fees/rebates, impact, latency, and cancellation.

## Off-policy evaluation cautions

Evaluating a new policy from data generated by another policy requires sufficient action overlap/support and correct adjustment for the data-collection policy. Importance weights can have extreme variance when actions are rarely observed. Doubly robust estimators still depend on overlap and at least one correctly specified component under their assumptions. Simulator-based evaluation adds model risk. Report effective sample size of weights, support diagnostics, sensitivity, and a conservative prospective test plan; do not rely on a high backtest score alone.
